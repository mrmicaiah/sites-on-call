Last week I searched for "plumber Decatur AL" and called the first three businesses that showed up.

The first guy answered on the second ring. Professional greeting, asked what I needed, offered to come out same day.

The second number went to a full voicemail box.

The third? Disconnected.

Here's the thing — all three of these businesses had a Google Business Profile. All three had reviews. All three *thought* they were showing up when people searched for plumbers in Decatur.

Two of them were wrong.

That gap — between what a contractor assumes is out there and what Google shows when someone searches your business — is the most expensive blind spot in the trades. Not because it's hard to fix. Because nobody looks.

## The Lie Contractors Tell Themselves

"I get most of my work from referrals."

I hear this constantly. And for a lot of contractors, it's true. Word of mouth built your business. Your reputation in the community keeps the phone ringing. You've never needed to "do marketing."

But here's what that statement actually means: you have no idea what happens when someone who *doesn't* know you tries to find you.

That homeowner who just moved to Madison from out of state? They're not asking their neighbor for a roofer recommendation — they don't know their neighbor yet. They're typing "roofing contractor Madison AL" into Google at 9pm while watching TV.

And if you're not showing up? They're calling your competitor. The one with the website. The one with 47 reviews. The one who bothered to claim their Google listing and keep it updated.

Referrals are a great business. They're a terrible growth plan, for the simple reason that they cap out at the size of the room you're known in. I wrote more about that ceiling in [why word of mouth isn't enough](/articles/why-word-of-mouth-isnt-enough/), but the short version: referral work is the business you already have. Search is the business you don't.

## What "Online Presence" Actually Means

When marketing people talk about "online presence," contractors' eyes glaze over. Sounds like buzzword nonsense.

So let me be specific. Your online presence is the answer to one question:

**What shows up when someone Googles your business name?**

Not "plumber near me." Your actual business name. The thing painted on your truck.

Try it right now. Open an incognito window (important — regular Google personalizes results based on your search history) and type in your business name plus your city.

What do you see?

### The Best Case Scenario

You see:
- Your Google Business Profile on the right side with your correct phone number, address, hours, and photos
- Your website as the first organic result
- Maybe your Facebook page
- A few directory listings (Yelp, BBB, Angi) that all show the same correct information

This is rare. Maybe 1 in 10 contractors I research have this.

### The More Common Scenario

You see:
- A Google Business Profile with your old phone number from 2019
- No website (or a GoDaddy placeholder page you forgot about)
- Three different addresses across different directory sites
- A Yelp page you never claimed with 2 stars from one angry customer
- Your competitor's Google ad at the very top

### The Worst Case

Nothing. Your business doesn't exist on the internet. The only thing that shows up is a whitepages listing and someone else's business with a similar name.

I've seen established contractors — guys doing $800K a year in revenue — who are essentially invisible online. They've survived on referrals and repeat customers for so long they never noticed.

## The Anatomy of Your Results Page

Here's where most articles on this topic stop being useful. They tell you to "check your online presence" and leave you staring at a screen full of boxes, not knowing which ones matter.

So let's take that page apart. When someone searches your business name, Google assembles a results page out of separate components, and each one is fed by a different source. Knowing which is which is the difference between "my Google looks bad" and "I know exactly which of six things is broken."

### The Knowledge Panel

That box on the right side of the screen — your name, phone, hours, photos, a map, a star rating — is your Google Business Profile rendered onto the search page. On a phone it isn't on the right; it's the first thing under the search bar, which is where the majority of your customers will see it.

This panel is the single most valuable piece of real estate you own on the internet, and it costs nothing. It's also the only element on the page you control directly and completely.

What to look at, in order:

**The phone number.** Tap it. Does it ring your phone? I'm not being condescending — this fails more often than you'd guess, and it fails silently.

**The hours.** Google will show "Closed" or "Open now" in real time. If your hours are wrong, Google is actively telling customers you're closed while you're standing in a truck holding a wrench.

**The categories.** Your primary category is one of the strongest ranking signals for local search, and most contractors set it once and never revisit it. A company that installs and services HVAC but is categorized only as "Air conditioning repair service" is invisible for installation searches.

**The photos.** Profiles with recent photos look like active businesses. Profiles whose newest photo is four years old look like a business that might not exist anymore.

**The "Questions & answers" section.** Anyone can post a question on your profile, and anyone can answer it — including your competitors, and including people who are wrong. Most contractors have never scrolled down far enough to know this section exists on their own listing.

If you want the full walkthrough of setting this up properly, I broke it down in [Google Business Profile for contractors](/articles/google-business-profile-for-contractors/).

### The Map Pack

Search a service instead of your name — "gutter repair Athens AL" — and near the top you'll get a map with three businesses under it. That's the map pack, and for local service searches it's where the phone calls come from.

Three slots. That's the whole game. Fourth place is a link that says "More places," and almost nobody clicks it.

The map pack is ranked on a different recipe than the blue links below it: relevance to what was searched, distance from where the searcher is standing, and prominence — Google's read on how established and well-regarded you are. You can't move your shop, but the other two are yours to work on. That's the mechanics I walk through in [local SEO for contractors](/articles/local-seo-for-contractors/).

### The Organic Results

The standard blue links. This is where your actual website lives or doesn't.

The thing contractors miss: Google ranks *pages*, not websites. Having a website doesn't get you anywhere for "deck building Huntsville." Having a page about deck building in Huntsville does. A five-page site with a real page per service will consistently beat a beautiful one-page site, because the one-pager has given Google exactly one thing to rank.

### People Also Ask

That expanding accordion of related questions in the middle of the page. It's worth reading even if you never try to appear in it, because it's a free transcript of what your customers are actually confused about. If "how much does it cost to replace gutters" is sitting in the People Also Ask box for your trade, that's a page you should have and probably don't.

### Review Snippets and the Star Rating

Google pulls a star rating into the results and, on some listings, a line or two from a review. You don't choose which review. Google does. Which means a single articulate one-star complaint can end up as the first sentence a stranger reads about your business, sitting there for years.

You can't delete it. What you can do is bury it under volume and recency, which is the entire practical argument for asking for reviews consistently rather than in panicked bursts.

### The "From the web" Box

Some profiles show quotes pulled from third-party review sites. This is the mechanism by which a Yelp page you never claimed and haven't looked at since 2019 gets to speak on your behalf on Google's results page. Worth checking, because you might be surprised who's talking.

### Related Searches and "People also search for"

At the bottom of the page, and inside the knowledge panel, Google lists businesses it considers similar to yours. Read that list carefully — it's Google telling you, out loud and for free, who it thinks your competitors are. Sometimes the list is wrong in a way that's diagnostic. If Google thinks a general handyman is your closest match and you're a specialty roofer, your categories and content aren't communicating what you actually do.

## Why NAP Consistency Matters (And What That Even Means)

NAP stands for Name, Address, Phone number. Boring, I know. But Google uses this information [across dozens of websites to verify that your business is real and legitimate](/articles/local-seo-for-contractors/).

When your NAP is inconsistent — your Google listing says "123 Main Street" but Yelp says "123 Main St" and the BBB says "123 Main Street, Suite A" — Google gets confused.

Is this one business or three? Are any of these addresses current? Which phone number actually works?

Google's response to confusion is always the same: show someone else instead.

I pulled a report for an HVAC company in Huntsville last month. They had 14 different directory listings. Seven of them had their old address from before they moved in 2022. Three had a phone number that now belongs to a pizza shop. Their Google Business Profile had the right information, but all those inconsistent listings were telling Google not to trust it.

They couldn't figure out why they'd dropped from the top 3 in local search results to page 2. This was why.

The reason this is worth caring about, and the reason it's so tedious to fix: you didn't create most of those listings. Data aggregators scrape business information and syndicate it out to directory sites automatically. Your business got listed in places you've never heard of, using information that was accurate whenever it got scraped. Move once, change one number, and you've seeded a dozen wrong records you'll never be notified about.

## The Review Problem Nobody Talks About

You know reviews matter. Everyone knows reviews matter. What contractors don't realize is *which* reviews matter and *where* they matter.

Your Google reviews are the only ones that directly affect your Google ranking. That's it. Yelp reviews affect your Yelp ranking. Facebook recommendations affect your Facebook visibility. But for showing up when someone searches "electrician near me"? Google reviews are the whole game.

So when you ask a happy customer to "leave a review" without being specific, and they post it on Facebook instead of Google, you've wasted an opportunity.

Here's the other thing: review velocity matters as much as total count.

A contractor with 89 reviews, all from 2021-2023, looks abandoned compared to a competitor with 34 reviews, 8 of them from the last 3 months. Google sees recency as a signal that you're still active and still doing good work.

One landscaper in Athens had 127 Google reviews — impressive number. But the most recent one was 14 months old. His profile had started dropping in rankings, and he blamed "Google changing the algorithm."

The algorithm didn't change. He stopped asking for reviews after he hit 100.

The fix is unglamorous and it works: ask every satisfied customer, at the moment the job is done and they're happy, and send them a direct link to the Google review form rather than telling them to "look us up." Every extra step between the ask and the review is a place where it doesn't happen.

## Your Website Probably Isn't Doing What You Think

"I have a website" isn't a yes-or-no question. It's a spectrum.

On one end: [a professional, mobile-friendly site](/articles/contractor-website-design/) with dedicated pages for each service you offer, a clear way to contact you, and fresh content that gives Google a reason to keep indexing it.

On the other end: a single page you paid your nephew $200 to make in 2018, with a stock photo of someone else's work truck and a contact form that emails an address you no longer check.

Both of these are technically "having a website." They are not the same thing.

Here's how I quickly evaluate a contractor's website:

**Load time**: Pull it up on your phone over cellular, not WiFi. Does it take more than 3 seconds? That's too slow. 40% of people will leave before it finishes loading.

**Mobile layout**: Same test. Does everything fit on the screen? Can you tap the phone number to call? Can you find the contact page in under 5 seconds?

**Copyright date**: Scroll to the footer. If it says "© 2021" we're in 2026 — your site looks abandoned. This is a 10-second fix that most people never make.

**Content depth**: Do you have individual pages for each service, or is everything crammed onto one page? Google ranks *pages*, not websites. If you want to show up for "deck building Huntsville" you need a page specifically about deck building in Huntsville.

**Last update**: When's the last time anything changed on this site? A blog post, a new project photo, anything? A site that hasn't been touched in 2 years tells Google (and customers) that you might not be in business anymore.

**Contact form**: Fill it out yourself. Send it. Does the email arrive? Broken contact forms are the quietest way to lose work there is — no bounce, no error, no way to know. Test yours twice a year.

## The Competitors You Don't Know About

Here's an exercise that makes contractors uncomfortable:

Search for "[your service] [your city]" — not your business name, just what a customer would type.

Write down the first 5 businesses that appear (skip the paid ads at the top).

Now ask yourself: Is your business on that list?

If not, those 5 companies are getting calls that could be yours. Not because they do better work. Not because they've been around longer. Because they show up and you don't.

I did this for "gutter installation Hartselle" last month. The number one organic result was a company from Birmingham — 80+ miles away. They outranked every local gutter company because they had a page specifically targeting "gutter installation Hartselle AL" with 600 words of content about why Hartselle homeowners need seamless gutters.

The actual Hartselle gutter companies? One had no website. Two had websites that didn't mention gutters specifically (just "home services"). One had a decent site but hadn't posted anything new since 2023.

Birmingham company is getting those calls.

And that's not one unlucky search. We pulled Google's first page for six common contractor searches across Huntsville, Madison, Decatur, and Athens and categorized all 53 results. Only 18 of them — about one in three — were an independent local contractor's own website. Directories and review platforms took 21 of the 53, and Yelp appeared on page one for all six trades. The full breakdown is in our [North Alabama contractor search study](/articles/north-alabama-contractor-search-study/), including the part worth ending on: in the Decatur roofing search, five of nine results were local roofers' own sites. They'd built real websites with real depth, and they owned their own town's search results because of it.

The spots are winnable. They're just empty.

## The Ten-Minute Self-Audit

You can find most of what's broken without any tools and without paying anyone. Set a timer, open an incognito window, and work through these in order. Write down what you find — the writing-down part matters, because the whole failure mode here is looking at something wrong and then forgetting about it.

**1. Search your business name plus your city.** Is your knowledge panel there? Is your website the first organic result, or is a directory sitting above you on your own name?

**2. Tap your own phone number** in the knowledge panel. Does it ring the phone in your pocket?

**3. Check your hours and your primary category.** Are the hours right for today? Does the category describe the work you actually want to be called for?

**4. Scroll to your reviews and look at the date on the newest one.** Not the count — the date. If it's older than about three months, that's your highest-value fix and it costs nothing but asking.

**5. Scroll further, to Questions & Answers.** Is there anything there? Is it accurate? Did you write it?

**6. Search your business name plus the word "reviews."** This surfaces the third-party pages talking about you — Yelp, BBB, Angi, Facebook. Look at what's there and note anything you've never claimed.

**7. Now search your service plus your city**, the way a stranger would. Are you in the map pack's three slots? Are you on page one at all? Write down who is.

**8. Open your own website on your phone, on cellular.** Time it. Check the footer year. Try to find your phone number in under five seconds.

**9. Submit your own contact form.** Wait for the email.

**10. Look at "People also search for"** in your knowledge panel. Does Google understand what kind of business you are?

Most contractors who do this honestly find between two and four things wrong. Almost all of them are fixable in an afternoon, and none of them require hiring anybody.

## What To Do About Each Thing You Found

An audit that ends in a list of problems is just a bad mood. Here's the actual repair order for the things the ten minutes usually turns up, roughly in the sequence I'd tackle them.

### "My phone number is wrong somewhere"

Fix Google first, because Google is where the calls come from. Sign into your Business Profile and correct it there before you touch anything else.

Then go after the directories, and go after them in the right order. Your listings on the big platforms — Yelp, BBB, Angi, Facebook, Apple Maps, Bing Places — are each editable directly, and those are worth doing by hand because they're the ones that show up on your name search. The long tail of sites you've never heard of mostly got their information from data aggregators, so correcting the aggregator record is more efficient than chasing fifty individual listings.

Set expectations: syndicated corrections take weeks to propagate, not hours. You will fix a listing and see the old one still sitting there ten days later. That's normal and it isn't broken.

One thing people skip: if you changed numbers, don't just update the listings. Keep the old number forwarding for a good while if you can. Every business card, truck magnet, and yard sign you've ever put out is still carrying it.

### "I have duplicate listings"

Duplicates split your reviews and your ranking signals across two records, which is worse than having one imperfect listing. On Google, the fix is to claim both and request removal of the duplicate — Google will merge the reviews in most cases rather than delete them.

Do this before a review push, not after. Otherwise you spend three months building reviews on the record that's going to get merged away.

### "There's an unclaimed Yelp page with a bad review on it"

Claim it. Claiming costs nothing and it does two things: you can correct the business information, and you can respond publicly to the review.

Respond to it even if it's years old, and especially if it's the one Google is quoting on your name search. Not defensively — a short, specific, non-argumentative reply. You're not writing to the angry customer, who is gone. You're writing to the stranger reading it in 2027 who is deciding whether to call you. A calm reply under a hostile review reads as competence. No reply reads as either "he didn't care" or "she's not in business anymore."

Then leave it alone. Arguing in review replies has never once worked.

### "My hours or categories are wrong"

Two-minute fix, disproportionate payoff.

For hours: set them accurately, and use the special-hours field for holidays instead of leaving Google to guess. If you take emergency calls after hours, that's what the profile description and your website should say — don't set your listed hours to 24/7 to game it, because customers will call at 11pm and be annoyed when nobody answers.

For categories: your primary category is doing the heaviest lifting, so it should describe the work you most want to be called for, not the broadest thing you're capable of. Add secondary categories for everything else you actually do. Go look at what the businesses currently in the map pack for your service are using — Google will tell you what it expects for your trade if you look at who it's already rewarding.

### "My newest review is a year old"

This is usually the highest-value item on the whole list, and it's free.

Ask in person, at the end of the job, while the customer is standing there happy. Not by email three days later. Then remove every step between the ask and the review: Google generates a short review link for your profile, and that link belongs in a saved text message on your phone so you can send it before you're off the property.

Don't buy reviews and don't run a review-gating scheme where you screen for happy customers first — both violate platform policies and both are detectable. Just ask everybody, consistently. A steady trickle beats a burst, because velocity is part of what Google is reading.

### "I'm nowhere in the map pack"

Map pack ranking runs on relevance, distance, and prominence. Distance you can't change. So you work the other two.

Relevance means your profile and your website actually say what you do, in the words customers use. Prominence is the aggregate of reviews, citations, and whether the rest of the web treats you as a real established business.

The honest caveat: if you're physically twenty minutes outside the city you're trying to rank in, the map pack for that city is a hard fight and may not be the right target. Your organic pages are the better play there. Don't burn a year trying to win a proximity fight you're geographically disqualified from.

### "My website is slow, dated, or missing pages"

Triage in this order, because the cheap fixes are also the high-impact ones:

First, the footer year and the contact form. Minutes of work. A dead form is actively losing you jobs right now.

Second, mobile and speed. Most contractor sites are slow because of oversized images — photos straight off a phone at full resolution. Compressing them is often the entire fix.

Third, pages. This is the real work and the real return. One page per service, one page per city you serve, each with enough substance to be worth ranking. That Birmingham gutter company beat every local outfit in Hartselle with 600 words on a page. Not a redesign. A page.

If you're weighing whether the site you have is worth repairing or replacing, [what good contractor websites actually look like](/articles/contractor-website-examples/) is a more useful yardstick than any sales pitch, mine included.

### "Everything looks fine"

Then say so out loud and stop worrying about it.

Some contractors run this audit and genuinely come out clean — profile accurate, reviews current, site working, ranking where they should be. If that's you, the correct next move is nothing. Put a reminder on the calendar to re-check in six months and go back to work.

Nobody in marketing ever tells you that you're fine. I'm telling you it's a real outcome, and it's more common than the industry's fear-based sales pitch would suggest.

## The Stuff You Can't See From Your Own Chair

Here's what drives me crazy about generic "improve your online presence" articles:

They tell you to "claim your Google Business Profile" — but what if you claimed it in 2019 and forgot the login?

They tell you to "get more reviews" — but what if you have reviews on the wrong platforms?

They tell you to "build a website" — but what if you have one that's actually hurting you?

The advice isn't wrong. It's just useless without knowing your specific situation.

The self-audit above gets you most of the way there. What it can't get you is the stuff that isn't visible from your own chair: the dozen directory records you never created, how you actually rank from across town rather than from your own driveway, what your competitors are doing that you aren't, and which of your problems is the one actually costing you calls.

## What To Do With This Information

You can't fix what you don't know is broken. That's the whole point of this article, and it's most of the value in it.

That roofer who's been at the same phone number for 15 years but has 4 different numbers listed online? He had no idea.

That plumber whose one-star Yelp review from 2020 is the third result when you Google her business name? She didn't know either.

That HVAC company outranked by a Birmingham competitor in their own city? They thought they were "doing fine online."

None of them were careless. They were busy running businesses. Checking what Google shows when someone searches your business is nobody's job until it's costing you money, and by then it's been costing you money for a while.

So do the ten-minute audit this week. Fix whatever it turns up — most of it is free. Ask your next three satisfied customers for a Google review, and send them the link instead of telling them to look you up.

Then decide, with actual information in front of you, whether you need anything more than that.

At minimum, you'll know. And knowing is better than guessing.
